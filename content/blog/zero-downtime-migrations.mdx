Most migration postmortems blame the wrong thing. It's rarely the database
copy itself — it's the assumptions baked into everything *around* it: cache
invalidation, background jobs still pointed at the old host, and clients that
cached a DNS answer longer than anyone remembered configuring.

## The checklist that actually matters

Before any cutover, we walk through the same four questions on every
engagement, regardless of stack:

1. **What still points at the old system by IP instead of hostname?**
   Load balancers, health checks, and cron jobs are the usual offenders.
2. **What has a TTL longer than your maintenance window?**
   DNS, CDN edge caches, and mobile app configs cached at install time.
3. **What's idempotent, and what isn't?**
   Replays during a rollback are inevitable — anything that isn't safe to
   run twice needs a guard before cutover day, not during it.
4. **Who gets paged if this goes sideways at 2am, and do they have the
   rollback runbook already open in a tab?**

None of this is exotic. It's just the difference between a migration that's
a non-event and one that becomes a war-room story.

> The migrations that go well are the boring ones — the runbook was
> rehearsed enough times that cutover night felt like a formality, not an
> event.

## Shifting traffic in increments

The single highest-leverage change we make on every migration is refusing to
cut over all at once. Route 5% of traffic, watch error rate and latency for
fifteen minutes, then 25%, then 50%. If something's wrong, you find it while
it's still cheap to reverse.
